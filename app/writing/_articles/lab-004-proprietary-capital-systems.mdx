Lab 004 was the bigger sibling to Arasaka Neural Bastion.

Arasaka started as a machine learning and forex research project: data, models, automation scripts, MQL5 integration, and the question of whether a system could reject weak signals. Lab 004 pushed that idea into a fuller operating system for research: scheduled ingestion, feature generation, market regime detection, model routing, strategy construction, backtesting, portfolio risk aggregation, paper execution, broker reconciliation, live guard monitoring, and enough logs to inspect the system after the fact.

I eventually stopped the project.

That is not the same as calling it a failure. It did not become as performant as I hoped, and the operational evidence showed too much fragility to justify continuing live execution. But it still represents months of design, implementation, deployment, observation, and revision. It is one of the projects that taught me the most because it forced the difference between a promising model and a complete system into the open.

> This is a technical research case study, not financial advice. Lab 004 was an experimental system for engineering, machine learning, automation, and controlled live testing.

## Abstract

Lab 004 tested whether a modular H1 forex system could move from raw market data to model-driven positions and broker-aware execution without becoming a tangled research script.

The project produced a large operational record:

- 9,329 saved JSON report artifacts across pipeline modules
- 370 hourly `m9` paper/execution reports
- 6,506 `m9_loss_guard` monitor reports
- 63 daily training reports
- 63 risk reports
- 120 order attempts recorded by M9
- 88 successful M9 executions
- 170 recorded target-vs-broker mismatches
- 10 loss-guard breach closes
- 3 daily stop triggers

The conclusion was mixed. The architecture worked well enough to create evidence, but the evidence did not support continuing the system as live capital infrastructure.

## Research Question

The project began with a practical question:

> Can I build a forex ML system disciplined enough to ingest data, train models, construct positions, execute through MT5, manage risk, and explain itself afterwards?

There were smaller questions inside that:

- Can H1 data support a useful signal pipeline across six FX pairs?
- Can market regimes route signals more carefully than one general-purpose model?
- Can strategy rules reduce churn, weekend risk, and high-volatility exposure?
- Can live execution stay aligned with model targets?
- Can logs and reports make the system auditable after a bad run?

The answer was not a clean yes or no. The system could run, record, and self-criticize. The performance and reliability did not clear the bar.

## System Shape

The project was organized as a Python package called `lab004-propcap`, short for **Trueblood Labs Lab 004 - Proprietary Capital Systems**. Its shape was deliberately modular:

- `m0_broker`: MetaTrader 5 connection and broker-facing utilities
- `m1_data`: bar ingestion
- `m2_clean`: cleaning and validation
- `m3_features`: feature generation
- `m4_regimes`: market-regime detection
- `m5_signals`: model training, routing, inference, and signal generation
- `m6_strategy`: position construction and strategy rules
- `m7_backtest`: backtest engine
- `m8_risk`: portfolio aggregation and risk reporting
- `m9_paper`: paper execution, live execution boundary, and reconciliation
- `m9_loss_guard`: guard process for breached positions and daily stops

The important design choice was that model output never became the whole decision. A signal had to pass through regime logic, confidence thresholds, cooldowns, minimum hold periods, high-volatility filters, weekend rules, stop logic, broker constraints, and reconciliation.

That shape was useful even when the results disappointed me. It made the system harder to fool because the output left a trail.

## Trading Universe And Runtime

The H1 universe covered six forex pairs:

- `EURUSD`
- `GBPUSD`
- `USDJPY`
- `AUDUSD`
- `USDCAD`
- `USDCHF`

The system stored data in Parquet, generated features per symbol, wrote positions per symbol, and aggregated portfolio risk. It ran hourly execution checks and daily training/evaluation jobs. In the later operational snapshot, the config used:

- `skip_high_vol: true`
- `confidence_min: 0.34`
- `cooldown_bars: 24`
- `min_hold_bars: 24`
- `friday_no_new_entries_hour_utc: 20`
- `friday_flatten_hour_utc: 21`
- `monday_force_flat: true`
- `stop_loss_atr_mult: 2.0`
- `take_profit_atr_mult: 0.0`
- `max_hold_bars: 168`
- `lot_size: 0.20`

Those numbers tell part of the story. The system became more defensive over time. It moved away from optimistic trading behavior and toward filtering, flattening, pausing, and guard rails.

Those numbers were not terrible as research outputs, but they were not enough. A separate V2 assessment also found that the portfolio and backtest normalization were flawed because raw PnL was aggregated across pairs without putting every symbol onto a common unit system. That meant USDJPY could dominate portfolio statistics because its raw price scale is different from five-decimal pairs.

That finding changed how I read the whole project. Some positive-looking reports were real evidence of a running system, but not reliable evidence of a deployable edge.

## Runtime Progression

The project also taught me that runtime is part of research quality. If a system is supposed to run hourly and daily, its jobs need to be predictable.

<img
  src="/writing/lab004/pipeline-runtime-progression.svg"
  alt="Line chart showing Lab 004 report-derived run durations for execution, training, and risk jobs"
  className="rounded-md shadow-md"
/>

The hourly execution loop was usually measured in seconds to a little over a minute. Training took longer and varied more, as expected. Risk aggregation stayed comparatively small. This mattered because the system could fit into a practical schedule: hourly checks at `:05`, daily train/evaluate around `23:45`, and minutely loss-guard monitoring.

The useful lesson was not "the jobs were fast." It was that the jobs had become measurable. Once runtime is measurable, it can become an operational constraint instead of a vague feeling.

## Execution And Reconciliation

The M9 layer was where the project stopped being only a model pipeline.

<img
  src="/writing/lab004/execution-reconciliation.svg"
  alt="Chart showing Lab 004 M9 execution attempts, successful executions, disabled execution reports, and target-broker mismatches"
  className="rounded-md shadow-md"
/>

The final M9 report set recorded:

- 370 paper/execution reports
- 310 reports with order execution enabled
- 60 reports with order execution disabled
- 120 order attempts
- 88 successful executions
- 170 target-vs-broker mismatches
- 0 M9 report errors

That mismatch count is one of the reasons I stopped trusting the system as live infrastructure. A model can be approximately right and the system can still fail at the execution boundary. The broker position after a run matters more than the target position on disk.

Late in the run, the latest M9 reports on June 16 showed `execute_orders: false` even though the copied config still had `m9.execute_orders: true`. That suggests the project had operational pause behavior or a runtime override beyond the static config. That is the kind of fact a research write-up should keep visible.

## Loss Guard Monitoring

The loss guard was added because normal hourly execution was not enough. I wanted a smaller, more frequent process that could watch live positions and close breached trades.

<img
  src="/writing/lab004/loss-guard-monitor.svg"
  alt="Chart showing Lab 004 loss guard checks, breached position closes, and daily stop triggers"
  className="rounded-md shadow-md"
/>

The `m9_loss_guard` report set recorded:

- 6,506 guard reports
- 10 breached positions
- 10 guard closes
- 3 daily stop triggers
- 1 JSON report with an error

The plain `loss_guard.log` also captured earlier startup failures. There were repeated MT5 initialization errors, mostly `Terminal: Authorization failed`, plus a couple of `IPC timeout` failures. Later JSON reports showed the guard connecting again and writing clean checks, but those startup failures mattered. They showed that live monitoring is only as reliable as the terminal, session, and credentials underneath it.

This is the kind of engineering lesson that does not show up in a backtest.

## Why I Stopped It

I stopped Lab 004 because the project had reached a point where the evidence was more valuable than another round of optimism.

The main reasons were:

- Performance did not justify live continuation.
- Portfolio metrics were not trustworthy enough until PnL normalization was fixed.
- Broker-target mismatches were too frequent.
- Execution state could diverge from config state.
- Loss-guard reliability depended on MT5 session health.
- High-volatility market coverage still needed a better model or policy.
- The reporting layer was strong enough to show the weaknesses clearly.

That last point is important. I did not stop it because there was no work left. I stopped it because the next work would have been a serious V2 rebuild, not a small polish pass.

## What The V2 Assessment Taught Me

The V2 assessment was the most useful document the project produced. It identified the work that would have needed to happen before I could responsibly treat the system as more than a research experiment:

- normalize PnL to pips, returns, or USD-equivalent values before portfolio aggregation
- harden broker execution and post-trade reconciliation
- add symbol-level execution capability discovery
- validate stop distances against broker constraints before sending orders
- report walk-forward model diagnostics by symbol and regime
- cover high-volatility regimes instead of suppressing a large slice of the market
- distinguish normal market closure from unexpected missing bars
- separate `row_state`, `entry_reason`, and `exit_reason` in strategy reports

The project did not need a prettier chart. It needed stronger measurement.

## What Still Worked

Even with the final decision to stop, several parts of the project worked as intended:

- The module boundaries made the system easier to inspect.
- Scheduled jobs produced a consistent operational record.
- Report artifacts gave me a research trail instead of relying on memory.
- M6 strategy rules made risk policy visible.
- M9 reports exposed broker mismatch problems directly.
- Loss guard reporting captured real risk-control behavior.
- The system produced enough evidence to argue against itself.

That is a real outcome. It is not the outcome I wanted at the start, but it is a good engineering outcome.

## The Difference From Arasaka

Arasaka asked:

> Can machine learning produce useful forex signals?

Lab 004 asked:

> Can a complete system ingest data, build signals, test them, size them, execute them, reconcile them, monitor them, and explain itself afterwards?

That second question is harder. It is also more honest. It forced me to see the entire chain: data, model, strategy, risk, broker, scheduler, logs, and human decision.

## Final Notes

Lab 004 did not become the capital system I hoped it would become.

But it did become a serious research project. It taught me how much of machine learning engineering lives outside the model. It taught me that logs are not an afterthought. It taught me that a system which can prove its own limits is more valuable than a demo that only shows its best day.

I am keeping it in the portfolio because it represents the kind of work that is easy to underestimate from the outside: months of small decisions, late fixes, failed assumptions, runbooks, metrics, scheduled tasks, guard rails, and the discipline to stop when the evidence says stop.
